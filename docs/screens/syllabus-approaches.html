A · Closest to your syllabus

Weekly cadence, data first

13 weekly modules in your schedule's order, plus a Start-here and a Wrap-up. Each week opens with a short data lab, ends with a retrieval check, and the project runs alongside as four milestones.

Fits because
  • Your schedule already runs week by week with one chapter each p. 4–5
  • Retrieval checks scale to 120 students without grading load
  • Keeps every topic where your students' textbook puts it
Changes versus your syllabus
Adds a claim-critique task in weeks 6, 10 and 14 so O6 is assessed; moves the midterm off week 11.
Trade-offs
Can become chapter coverage; module objectives and a cumulative element in the final milestone guard against it.
Evidence, in one line
Weekly low-stakes quizzing with feedback: g ≈ 0.5 across 222 studies (Yang et al. 2021).

B · Built around your 40% project

Project-driven units with milestones

Five units that follow the project's own stages: Ask → Collect → Describe → Compare → Communicate. Each unit ends in a milestone with rubric feedback; textbook topics arrive when the project needs them.

Fits because
  • "The project is the point of the course" your answer, q. 5
  • O4 (design a study) and O5 (communicate) get authentic tasks instead of a multiple-choice midterm p. 3
  • Milestones spread the week-11 spike across four dates
Changes versus your syllabus
Chapters 3 and 4 swap (sampling before summaries); the midterm becomes milestone 2. Topics otherwise keep their order. See the sequence diff
Trade-offs
Four milestones × 120 students is real grading load; AI-drafted rubric feedback helps but you review each. Students new to open-ended work need scaffolding in unit 1.
Evidence, in one line
Project-based learning d ≈ 0.71 (Chen & Yang 2019); weaker short-term recall in problem-centred designs, so weekly retrieval checks stay.
7 modules · avg 7.9 h/wk · peak 8.8 hO1–O6 each assessed ≥ 2×
Draws on: backward design · Tessera standard 2.4, 3.3 · OSCQR 45, 47 · TILT for each milestone
C · Makes O6 the through-line

Two-week cases, each a claim to test

Seven two-week cases. Each opens with a statistical claim from the news, teaches the tools needed to test it, and closes with a short written critique. The project becomes the final case.

Fits because
  • "Less lecture, more 'what does this number mean'" your answer, q. 5
  • O6 is assessed every two weeks instead of never p. 2–3
  • Real data from week 1, as you asked
Changes versus your syllabus
Pairs your 13 topics into 7 cases; homework becomes the critique write-up; weights unchanged.
Trade-offs
Case discussion with 120 students needs sections or a structured online thread; more facilitation than A. You supply the claims: I leave a slot in each case, never an invented article.
Evidence, in one line
Problem-based designs favor long-term retention and skill over short-term recall (Strobel & van Barneveld 2009); the GAISE College Report recommends real data and statistical thinking over procedures.
9 modules · avg 8.1 h/wk · peak 9.6 hO6 assessed 7×
Draws on: Merrill's first principles · Tessera standard 3.1 · OSCQR 29, 44
